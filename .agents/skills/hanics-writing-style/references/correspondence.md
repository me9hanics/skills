# Correspondence

## Cover letters and applications

Build relevance through a coherent account of the writer's experience and thinking. A useful progression is:

**Relevant context or experience → concrete work and its mechanics → what it taught or changed → relevance to this opportunity.**

Use the parts supported by the material, in the order the letter needs. This is a narrative aid, not a mandatory paragraph template.

- Establish the purpose and specific connection to the recipient early.
- Describe selected work concretely enough to show what was done and why it matters. Connect it to the opportunity through an actual overlap rather than a generic claim of fit.
- Preserve personal reflection when it carries information: how the writer entered an unfamiliar domain, discovered an interest, revised an assumption, or developed a way of working.
- Distinguish demonstrated capability, adjacent experience, and willingness to learn. Express interest through specific reasons and encounters. Do not manufacture enthusiasm or infer expertise from interest.
- Use direct wording and varied sentence lengths. Avoid bloated paragraph openings and lengthy framing such as "in terms of" when simpler wording carries the meaning. For cover letters, use sentence breaks or ordinary punctuation instead of em dashes.
- End with a natural next step appropriate to the opportunity. Respect any supplied length, signature, or format requirements; this style does not prescribe a fixed page count or signature.

Illustrative reflection: "The project taught me how to enter an unfamiliar domain and contribute to it. I would bring that approach to your team's work." Use such a connection only when the underlying experience and intent are supplied; retain more specific detail when available.

## Emails and outreach

Give the reader the necessary context, reason for writing, and request promptly. A short practical message may need only those elements. For outreach resembling a cover letter, use the relevant narrative guidance above without expanding it into a full application.

Warmth should serve the relationship: a personal connection, a brief thanks, or good wishes may belong. Preserve these when meaningful; avoid piling up interchangeable pleasantries. Keep the ask clear and proportionate, such as a question, feedback request, or invitation to discuss.

Illustrative distinction:

> My background is in research and data engineering, with an interest in life sciences and graph-based solutions. What level of experience is the team looking for?

The interests remain interests. Rewriting them as professional background would change the claim.
